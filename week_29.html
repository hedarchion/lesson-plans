Monday DLP — Speaking Focus

I1D • 2026-08-17 • CS 2.1 / LS 2.1.1
Session 1 of 3 sunny · rainy · cloudy · windy · hot · cold · umbrella · season

FIRST session of a 3-session unit on 'Weather and Seasons'. Introduces the unit vocabulary that later sessions will build on.

<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="text-decoration: underline;"><strong>Set Induction</strong></span></p>
<p>1. Teacher provides simple verbal clues for weather types, such as 'I feel very hot' or 'I see water falling from the sky'.<br>2. A volunteer points to the corresponding word on the board from the unit word bank: sunny, rainy, cloudy, windy, hot, cold, umbrella, or season.<br>3. The class performs a choral repetition of the guessed word to reinforce pronunciation.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="text-decoration: underline;"><strong>Lesson Development</strong></span></p>
<p><strong>Weather Vocabulary and Sentence Frames</strong></p>
<p>1. Teacher displays the unit word bank and introduces the sentence frame: 'It is [weather]. I need a/an [object].'<br>2. The class practices reading the sentence frames aloud using different weather words from the list.<br>3. Teacher models how to combine weather and feelings, such as 'It is windy and I feel cold.'</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>Weather Report Simulation</strong></p>
<p>1. Pupils work in pairs to simulate a short weather forecast using a cardboard microphone prop.<br>2. One student says 'Today it is rainy' while the other student holds an umbrella to demonstrate the meaning.<br>3. Each pair takes turns performing their 2-sentence simulation for the class to practice speaking skills.<br>4. Teacher provides immediate verbal scaffolding for pupils who struggle with the sentence structure.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>Weather Poster Gallery Walk</strong></p>
<p>1. Each small group creates a simple poster by drawing one weather condition and writing a matching sentence from the word bank.<br>2. Groups display their posters around the classroom walls.<br>3. The class walks around the room in their groups to view the work of their peers.<br>4. Pupils leave a small smiley face sticky note on posters where the sentence and the drawing match correctly.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="text-decoration: underline;"><strong>Post-lesson</strong></span></p>
<p>1. Each pupil completes an exit ticket by whispering one sentence about their favorite weather to the teacher before leaving.<br>2. Teacher tells the class to remember these words because in the next session, they will learn how these weather types change across different seasons.</p>

Reflection

Dharshayani A/P Paul and Mohamad Ryyan showed excellent confidence during the weather report simulation. Muhamad Wafy Hau successfully named five words from the word bank and used them in the sentence frames with minimal teacher assistance.

Tuesday DLP — Reading Focus

S1D • 2026-08-18 • CS 3.1 / LS 3.1.1
Session 2 of 3 sunny · rainy · cloudy · windy · hot · cold · umbrella · season

SECOND session of the 3-session unit on 'Weather and Seasons'. Builds on Session 1 (Weather vocabulary (sunny, rainy, cloudy, windy)). Must OPEN with a recap of the unit vocabulary from Session 1.

<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="text-decoration: underline;"><strong>Set Induction</strong></span></p>
<p>1. Teacher draws a large circle on the board and writes the word 'Weather' in the center to start a word web.<br>2. Teacher asks the class to recall the unit words learned in the previous session such as 'sunny' and 'rainy'.<br>3. Volunteers come forward to write other words like 'cloudy', 'windy', 'hot', 'cold', 'umbrella', and 'season' on the board to complete the recap.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="text-decoration: underline;"><strong>Lesson Development</strong></span></p>
<p><strong>Weather Sentence Relay Race</strong></p>
<p>1. Teacher divides the class into two teams and places flashcards with the unit words 'sunny', 'rainy', 'windy', and 'cloudy' at the front.<br>2. Teacher reads a short sentence aloud, such as 'It is hot and sunny today'.<br>3. The first pupil from each team races to the board to touch the correct weather word mentioned in the sentence.<br>4. Teacher leads the class in choral repetition of each sentence to reinforce the connection between the spoken and written word.<br>5. The game continues until all pupils have had a turn to identify a weather word from the unit word bank.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>Think-Pair-Share Reading Task</strong></p>
<p>1. Teacher displays a short text: 'Today is a rainy day. It is cold outside. I need my umbrella. I do not like this season.'<br>2. Teacher explains that the 'main idea' is the most important part of the story and 'details' are extra information.<br>3. Pupils think individually to find the main idea and two supporting details using a provided sentence frame.<br>4. Each pair discusses their answers and helps each other write the words into a simple graphic organizer.<br>5. A volunteer from one pair reads their findings to the class to check for understanding.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="text-decoration: underline;"><strong>Post-lesson</strong></span></p>
<p>1. Each pupil identifies one thing they found easy and one thing they found hard during the reading and writing activities today.<br>2. Teacher informs the class that in the next session, they will use these sentences to create a weather forecast poster for the final unit project.</p>

Reflection

Dharshayani A/P Paul and Nick Mikhael were able to identify the main idea and supporting details with minimal guidance. Santhiya A/P Subramaniam required additional scaffolding and choral repetition to recognize the word 'umbrella' in the text.

Pupils turn to their partners and take turns explaining what they remember about the different weather types and seasons discussed in the previous two sessions.<br>3. The teacher calls on a few pupils to share one weather word their partner mentioned to consolidate the unit vocabulary before the writing task.</p>

<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="text-decoration: underline;"><strong>Lesson Development</strong></span></p>

In pairs, pupils take turns role-playing as weather reporters, using the frames to describe different seasons and weather types from the word bank.<br>3. <p>&nbsp;</p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="text-decoration: underline;"><strong>Post-lesson</strong></span></p>

Monday DLP — Speaking Focus

I1E • 2026-08-17 • CS 2.1 / LS 2.1.1
Session 1 of 3 price · cheap · expensive · buy · pay · change · shop · how much

FIRST session of a 3-session unit on 'Shopping and Money'. Introduces the unit vocabulary that later sessions will build on.

<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="text-decoration: underline;"><strong>Set Induction</strong></span></p>
<p>1. Teacher displays three zoomed-in mystery images on the screen: a close-up of a barcode, a silver coin, and a brightly colored price tag.<br>2. Pupils work in pairs to guess what each image represents and how they relate to a common daily activity.<br>3. The teacher reveals the full images and elicits the topic of 'Shopping and Money' while introducing the lesson goal of conducting a shop role play.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="text-decoration: underline;"><strong>Lesson Development</strong></span></p>
<p><strong>Vocabulary Board Relay</strong></p>
<p>1. Teacher writes the unit word bank (price, cheap, expensive, buy, pay, change, shop, how much) on the board and conducts a choral drill for pronunciation.<br>2. The class is divided into four teams, and the teacher provides a definition or a synonym for one of the target words.<br>3. One representative from each team races to the board to circle the correct word and use it in a short sentence.<br>4. The activity continues until all teams have practiced using all 8 words from the word bank in spoken and written contexts.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>Shop Stall Gallery Walk</strong></p>
<p>1. Each pair of pupils creates a 'mini-shop' display at their desk using three items (e.g., stationery or snacks) and labels them with prices.<br>2. Half of the pupils act as shopkeepers, while the other half act as customers walking around the room to different stalls.<br>3. Customers must ask at least 3 questions using the target vocabulary, such as 'How much is this?' and 'Is there a cheaper price?'<br>4. Shopkeepers answer using full sentences, incorporating words like 'pay', 'change', and 'expensive'.<br>5. After visiting a stall, customers leave a peer feedback sticky note indicating if the shopkeeper successfully used the unit word bank words.<br>6. Pupils swap roles after 10 minutes to ensure everyone practices speaking as both buyer and seller.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="text-decoration: underline;"><strong>Post-lesson</strong></span></p>
<p>1. Pupils complete a 3-2-1 exit task where they write down 3 new words they used, 2 questions they asked their peers, and 1 opinion on their shopping experience.<br>2. Teacher provides a curiosity hook by announcing that in the next session, they will use these same words to solve a 'mystery receipt' puzzle involving missing prices.</p>

Reflection

Ainnur Raudhah and Chai Zi Jian showed high proficiency by using complex connectors like 'although' when discussing prices. Krettisha Nair successfully met the learning objective by asking four distinct questions using all eight words from the unit word bank.

Wednesday DLP — Reading Focus

R1E • 2026-08-19 • CS 3.1 / LS 3.1.1
Session 2 of 3 price · cheap · expensive · buy · pay · change · shop · how much

SECOND session of the 3-session unit on 'Shopping and Money'. Builds on Session 1 (Items and prices vocabulary -- shop role play). Must OPEN with a recap of the unit vocabulary from Session 1.

<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="text-decoration: underline;"><strong>Set Induction</strong></span></p>
<p>1. Teacher welcomes the class and reminds them of the items and prices discussed in the shop role play during the previous session.<br>2. Teacher displays three sentences on the board with blanks: 'The apple is ___, not expensive,' 'I want to ___ a shirt at the shop,' and 'How much ___ do I get back?'<br>3. Pupils volunteer to fill in the blanks using the unit word bank (cheap, buy, change) to recap the vocabulary from Session 1.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="text-decoration: underline;"><strong>Lesson Development</strong></span></p>
<p><strong>Analyzing a Shopping Text</strong></p>
<p>1. Teacher displays a short paragraph: 'Yesterday, I went to the local shop to buy some snacks. I found a bag of chips for RM3 and a soda for RM2. The total price was RM5, which was quite cheap. I had to pay with a RM10 note and received RM5 in change.'<br>2. Teacher explains how to find the main idea (the purpose of the trip) and supporting details (specific items, prices, and the change received).<br>3. Teacher demonstrates how to rewrite a detail like 'The total price was RM5' into 'The person spent five ringgit' to show understanding in their own words.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>Detail Hunt Relay Race</strong></p>
<p>1. Teacher divides the class into four teams and sticks a different short shopping list text on the four corners of the room.<br>2. Teacher calls out a specific question such as 'How much was the total?' or 'Was the item expensive or cheap?'<br>3. One pupil from each team races to their assigned text, reads it, and runs back to write the answer on the board.<br>4. The team that correctly identifies and writes the most supporting details from their text wins the round.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>Think-Pair-Share: Receipt Summary</strong></p>
<p>1. Each pair is given a printed receipt showing three items: bread (RM4), milk (RM7), and butter (RM6).<br>2. Pairs must 'think' and 'pair' to identify the main idea and three supporting details regarding the prices and total cost.<br>3. Each pair writes a short 3-sentence paragraph in their own words explaining what was bought and if the price was high or low.<br>4. A few pairs share their written output with the class to verify they have used the unit words like 'pay' and 'expensive' correctly.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="text-decoration: underline;"><strong>Post-lesson</strong></span></p>
<p>1. Each pupil completes an individual reflection by writing down one thing that was easy to understand and one detail they found hard to explain in their own words.<br>2. Teacher provides a curiosity hook by asking, 'Now that we can read receipts, what happens if the shopkeeper gives you the wrong change?' to preview the final consolidation session.</p>

Reflection

Tuesday DLP — Reading Focus

S1M • 2026-08-18 • CS 3.1 / LS 3.1.1
Session 1 of 3 web app · AI tool · training data · predict · model · server · cloud · hallucination · bias · limitation

PBL PROJECT -- the class has built and presented an AI-powered English learning web app and discussed AI ethics. This week the project goes UNDER THE HOOD: pupils learn the technology behind the AI they used -- how LLMs learn, the infrastructure that runs them, and their known limitations.

<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="text-decoration: underline;"><strong>Set Induction</strong></span></p>
<p>1. Teacher displays a 5-question mini-quiz on the board covering vocabulary from the previous project phase, including words like 'web app' and 'AI tool'.<br>2. Pupils work in pairs to answer the questions on their mini-whiteboards within a three-minute time limit.<br>3. Pupils swap whiteboards with the pair next to them to self-check their answers as the teacher reveals the correct terms on the screen.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="text-decoration: underline;"><strong>Lesson Development</strong></span></p>
<p><strong>How LLMs Predict Text</strong></p>
<p>1. Teacher distributes a short reading passage: 'An AI model acts like a pattern-spotting engine. It processes massive amounts of training data from the internet to learn how language works. By identifying these patterns, the AI tool can predict the most likely next word in a sequence. However, because it relies on probability, a limitation is that it does not truly understand the meaning like a human does.'<br>2. Teacher explains the concept of 'predicting the next word' using a visual diagram of a sentence tree on the board.<br>3. Activity: Board Relay Race where the class is split into two teams. Teacher reads a definition (e.g., 'The information used to teach the AI'), and one pupil from each team races to write the correct word bank term (training data) on the board.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>Think-Pair-Share: Under the Hood</strong></p>
<p>1. In their project teams, pupils discuss how their specific AI-powered English learning app uses an AI model to generate responses for users.<br>2. Each pair writes a 2-3 sentence explanation of the process on a worksheet, ensuring they use at least 5 words from the word bank: web app, AI tool, training data, predict, model, server, cloud, hallucination, bias, limitation.<br>3. Pairs exchange their written explanations with another team to check if the technical process of using training data to predict text is described accurately.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="text-decoration: underline;"><strong>Post-lesson</strong></span></p>
<p>1. One student is selected to summarise how the AI model learns from training data to predict words, while other students are invited to add any missing technical details.<br>2. Teacher provides a curiosity hook: 'We now know how the AI brain thinks, but where does this brain actually live? Tomorrow, we explore the physical server and the cloud infrastructure that powers our apps.'</p>

Reflection

Aayra Faiz Firman and Ahmad Harraz showed excellent command of the technical vocabulary during the relay race. Muhammad Izzat was able to explain the concept of word prediction clearly in the written task, demonstrating a solid grasp of the A2 High reading objectives.

Wednesday DLP — Reading Focus

R1M • 2026-08-19 • CS 3.1 / LS 3.1.2
Session 2 of 3 web app · AI tool · training data · predict · model · server · cloud · hallucination · bias · limitation

SECOND session of the PBL tech arc. Builds on Session 1 (Tuesday): pupils understand how an LLM learns; this session explains WHERE and HOW the AI actually runs -- data centres, GPU servers, cloud platforms and the APIs that apps call. Must OPEN with a recap of the project word bank from Session 1.

<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="text-decoration: underline;"><strong>Set Induction</strong></span></p>
<p>1. Teacher displays word cards for 'web app', 'AI tool', 'training data', 'predict', and 'model' on the board to recap Session 1 concepts.<br>2. One pupil from each project team participates in a 'Slap-the-Board' game to match the teacher's definitions to the correct vocabulary cards.<br>3. Teacher introduces the day's focus by asking where these AI models actually live, leading into the concepts of 'server' and 'cloud'.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="text-decoration: underline;"><strong>Lesson Development</strong></span></p>
<p><strong>Reading and Identifying AI Infrastructure</strong></p>
<p>1. Teacher distributes a short technical text: 'To run an AI tool, we need a server, which is a powerful computer. Inside, GPUs do the math to predict text. These resources are hosted in the cloud, and developers use an API to connect the model to their web app.'<br>2. In project teams, pupils read the text and highlight the four infrastructure components: server, GPUs, cloud, and API.<br>3. Each team selects three components and drafts one sentence for each, defining its role based on the reading (e.g., 'The cloud stores the model so it can be accessed anywhere').<br>4. Teacher moves between groups to provide feedback on sentence structure and technical accuracy.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>Infrastructure Gallery Walk</strong></p>
<p>1. Teams create a visual 'AI Infrastructure Map' on mahjong paper, illustrating the flow from the cloud and server through the API to the user.<br>2. Pupils display their maps around the room and participate in a gallery walk to observe how other teams explained the infrastructure roles.<br>3. Each pupil must leave at least one sticky note on another team's work providing peer feedback on the clarity of their 'role sentences'.<br>4. One volunteer from each team remains at their station to explain their infrastructure choices to visiting peers using the project word bank.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="text-decoration: underline;"><strong>Post-lesson</strong></span></p>
<p>1. Pupils complete a 3-2-1 exit ticket: they write 3 infrastructure types learned, 2 questions they still have about the cloud, and 1 opinion on AI technology.<br>2. Teacher provides a curiosity hook for the next session by asking what happens when a server produces a 'hallucination' or 'bias' in its output.</p>

Reflection

Aayra Faiz Firman and Ahmad Harraz demonstrated high-level critical thinking by explaining the API's role as a bridge during the gallery walk. Fatin Aqilah successfully identified all three infrastructure types and wrote accurate sentences for each role.

S2E • 2026-08-18 • CS 3.1 / LS 3.1.1
Session 1 of 3 scan · main point · detail · evidence · notice · justify · paragraph · summarise · key word · conclusion

FIRST session of a 3-session 'Reading Detectives' unit. Pupils learn two reading powers (scanning for specific information, and separating the main point from details) and apply them to justify choices with textual evidence.

<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="text-decoration: underline;"><strong>Set Induction</strong></span></p>
<p>1. Teacher displays the 'Reading Detectives' title slide and reads the three 'You can...' outcomes aloud: read with a purpose, find the main point, and justify answers with evidence.<br>2. Retrieval warm-up: pupils are given 8 seconds to scan a short text on screen and answer one quick question (e.g., find the name, price or date). They experience the strategy before it is named.<br>3. Teacher asks pupils how they found the answer so quickly; pupils describe looking for key words or numbers. Teacher labels the strategy on the board: 'Scanning = looking for specific information quickly.'</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="text-decoration: underline;"><strong>Lesson Development</strong></span></p>
<p><strong>Two Reading Powers: Main Point vs Detail</strong></p>
<p>1. Teacher models big-picture reading: read the first and last sentences to get the gist, then demonstrate on a sample text on the slide.<br>2. Teacher introduces Power 1 (Main Point): what the text is mostly about, and Power 2 (Detail): the specific facts that support it.<br>3. Pupils sort statements about the text into 'Main Point' or 'Detail'. Teacher highlights the classic trap: a true fact is not automatically the main point.<br>4. Teacher writes the evidence frames on the board: 'The main point is that...' and 'One detail is... because the text says...'</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>Guided Practice: Evidence Hunt</strong></p>
<p>1. Pupils answer a comprehension question from the slide and must underline the exact sentence that proves their answer (textual evidence).<br>2. Distracter trap: the text contains a number that matches an answer option but is the wrong fact. Pupils must scan carefully and explain why the matching number is a trap.<br>3. Teacher reviews the answers and asks volunteers to read out their evidence sentence to the class.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>Application Mission: Choose a Destination</strong></p>
<p>1. Mission brief: pupils must choose a trip destination that meets four criteria (e.g., price, distance, activities, food) from a set of options.<br>2. In pairs, pupils scan the option cards and shortlist only the destinations that meet all four criteria.<br>3. Each pair defends its choice with four pieces of evidence, one per criterion, using the frame: 'We chose X because the text says...'</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>Independent Transfer: Picnic Notice</strong></p>
<p>1. Individually, pupils read a new picnic notice on the slide and find the main point, one detail, and one piece of evidence on their own.<br>2. This independent task doubles as the formative exit check; pupils hand in their answers as they finish.<br>3. Early finishers compare answers with a partner and explain which word in the notice gave them the main point.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>Extended Reading Practice: Detective Worksheet</strong></p>
<p>1. Pupils work on a reading worksheet with two short texts (35 minutes), applying the same powers: scanning questions plus a main-point/detail table for each text.<br>2. Teacher circulates, prompting pupils who struggle to separate a true fact from the main point to use the sentence frames from the board.<br>3. Worksheet answers are checked in pairs, then the class goes through the main-point rows together.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><span style="text-decoration: underline;"><strong>Post-lesson</strong></span></p>
<p>1. Recap: teacher runs a quick thumbs-up-thumbs-down check -- 'What is scanning?', 'Is a true fact always the main point?'<br>2. Each pupil tells their partner one thing they can now do as a reading detective, e.g., scan for a number or find the main point in a notice.<br>3. Teacher collects the picnic-notice exit slips and previews the next lesson: applying the same strategies to longer texts.</p>

Reflection

Leong Kah Kin and Nur Damia Qistina confidently sorted main point from detail in the sorting game and defended their trip choice with four pieces of evidence. Fawwaz Zahirul needed prompting in guided practice after falling for the number-matching distracter, but improved once he reread the evidence sentence using the frame. The class's strong retrieval record (90% average on the page-10 task) showed in the 8-second scanning challenge, while the main-point rows of the worksheet (weaker on page 62, 73%) confirmed that separating a true fact from the main point remains the key target for follow-up sessions.
